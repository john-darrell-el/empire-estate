Preface

This volume asks one question and answers it from the grantor’s law read against the grantee’s own record: when a sovereign grant of immoveable property is made and perfected under classical Mā-likī law, and the grantee’s own two-hundred-forty-year documentary record is read against that law, what does the law of the grant assign? The answer is the remedial path: the instrument, the parties and the class, the breach, the remedy, and the landscape and the closure.

The method is doctrinal construction under governing law, on documentary evidence.

The volume engages the classical Mālikī sources proven of record — the Muwaṭṭaʾ, the Mudawwana, Khalīl’s Mukhtaṣar, and Ibn Rushd’s Bidāyat al-Mujtahid, with their commen-tary chains — the instrument of 1786 and its renewal of 1836, and the grantee’s own documentary record as assembled and certified in Volume N of this series. It asserts nothing operational. No forum is selected, no procedure is invoked, and no program is administered; the volume states what the governing law assigns, and stops there.

Three conventions govern the text. First, governing-law primacy: the classical Mālikī sources control term-meaning in this volume, and the volume’s locked meanings — ṣadaqa, hiba, qabḍ, and their defined terms — are applied as defined, not as translated. Second, engagement at frozen loci: every assertion carries a frozen locus of record, and quotations are rationed to spans that carry load, verified against the certified components. Third, the standing subordination rule: grantee-side paper is quoted as evidence of grantee-side appli-cation and attitude; it is subordinate to the controlling hiba per ruling V-P. The doctrine register controls; the mapping register orients; no contemporary authority is doctrinally controlling.

1.2 The hierarchy of authority

The rulings of record fix five levels, and this chapter states them once, completely, because every later chapter stands on them.

First, the Mālikī sharīʿa hiba of 1786 is controlling.7 The instrument is a unilateral sovereign bestowal — a grant — and its meaning is the meaning the governing law gives it.

Second, the Emperor’s intention is immutable and controlling over all interpretation.8 A grant speaks its grantor’s intention; the grantee’s subsequent preferences about what the grant should have said are legally inert.

Third, immutability binds both directions. A hiba has three pillars:

A donee who neglected to take possession while the donor was in health, and who comes forward only when the donor has fallen ill, takes nothing — seizure is no longer permitted to him, because the law will not let a gift be perfected out of the estate once death-illness has intervened.17
