Chapter 1 The Emperor’s Declaration of 1777

On December 20, 1777, Sultan Sidi Muhammad bin Abdallah, Emperor of Morocco, issued a declaration to all consuls and merchants in the ports of Tangier, Salé, and Mogador. The declaration announced that all vessels sailing under the American flag could freely enter Moroccan ports. Americans were invited to “come and traﬀic freely in these ports in like manner as they formerly did under the English flag.”

This was not a diplomatic formality. It was a sovereign act of hospitality— the host opening his doors to a new guest. The timing is critical: the Decla-ration of Independence had been signed barely eighteen months earlier. The Continental Army was at Valley Forge. Most European powers had not yet recognized the United States. Morocco was the first.

The declaration was simultaneously sent to “all consuls and merchants”

in the Moroccan port cities, informing them that the Emperor had opened his ports to Americans and nine other European states with which Morocco had no existing treaties: Russia, Malta, Sardinia, Prussia, Naples, Hungary, Leghorn, Genoa, and Germany. The declaration was an act of sovereign prerogative—the Emperor deciding, unilaterally, who would be admitted to his dominions and under what terms.

Under Vattel’s Law of Nations, Book II, Chapter 8, Section 104: “The sovereign ought not to grant an entrance into his state for the purpose of drawing foreigners into a snare; as soon as he admits them, he engages to protect them as his own subjects, and to afford them perfect security, as far as depends on him.”

Vattel further observes: “We see that every sovereign who has given an asylum to a foreigner, considers himself no less offended by an injury done to the latter, than he would be by an act of violence committed on his own subject.”

The Emperor’s 1777 Declaration activated this obligation. Morocco became the host-creditor.

The United States became the guest-debtor.

The credit Volume J: The Host and the Guest extended was sovereign protection—and the debt incurred was the reciprocal obligation to honor the Emperor’s terms.

The Transfer Clause

The phrase “in like manner as they formerly did under the English flag” is the operative transfer clause. The Americans had been trading in Moroccan waters as sub-guests of Britain—operating under British tribute payments and British treaty protections. The Anglo-Moroccan alliance dated to 1585, when Queen Elizabeth I and Sultan Ahmad al-Mansur established commercial agreements on the basis of common enmity to Philip II of Spain. Formal treaties were concluded in 1721, 1728, 1734, 1750, and 1760. Sugar, ostrich feathers, and saltpeter from Morocco were exchanged for English fabrics and firearms. The establishment of the Barbary Company in 1585 gave England a monopoly on Morocco trade for twelve years.

When the Revolution severed the American tie to the English Crown, the Americans lost their sub-guest status. They could no longer trade in Moroccan waters under the British flag.
