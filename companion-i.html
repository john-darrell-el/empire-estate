0.2 Inference-label convention Every analytical statement in this volume carries one of three labels:

• RECORD — an archival fact with citation: the document exists, says this, and

— Chapter 1 · the opening —

Chapter 1 Purpose, Method, and the Second Lens

1.1 Why a companion Volume I of this series established the gateway framework: the demonstration that the Treaty of Peace and Friendship operates through three structural precondi-tions — nationality, the public record, and venue and performance — and that the treaty’s protections follow from gateway access, while the denial or collapse of gateway access withdraws them, whether by design or by external interference [1]. On that foundation Volume I read three case studies — Dungey, the Sundry Moors, and Dred Scott — as studies in treaty performance and non-performance: each case was placed against the gateways, the performance pathway was traced where the architecture held, and the collapse was traced where it did not [2]. Vol-ume I closed with the architecture proven, the gateways predictive, performance quiet, and collapse traceable [3].

What Volume I established, it established within a defined lens: the treaty as an architecture of mutual obligation between coequal signatory powers, its pro-visions performed or not performed as between the two parties. That lens is the bilateral lens, and it remains canonical and untouched.

Two things Volume I deliberately did not attempt. First, it did not read the cases through the grant doctrine developed in Volumes J through O of this series — the unilateral hiba sovereign grant, in which the Emperor’s instrument stands as a sovereign act of donation, the grantee’s position is construed against the grantee, and the treaty’s venue provisions operate as assignments rather than mere avail-abilities [4] (RECONSTRUCTION — the grant doctrine is the series’ doctrinal model, cited here as the companion’s operating frame). Second, Volume I expressly de-clined to assert the consular mechanism in the Sundry Moors matter as fact: its §3.7 states that “[t]he role of consular or diplomatic intermediation in the case of the Sundry Moors is a matter of analytical reconstruction” and that the framing is “offered as a treaty-aligned analytical model, not as an assertion of archival fact”

[5] (RECORD — quotation from the canonical text; the quoted sentence ends at the source’s own period, no terminal-punctuation adaptation). The point Volume I left open is the point at which this companion begins.

A companion, rather than a revision, is the form of this volume (the decision of record, §0.1.7). The frozen canon is preserved; the two-lens contrast is itself the scholarly contribution.

1.2 The two lenses The bilateral lens, of record in Volume I, treats the treaty as a reciprocal instru-ment: obligations run between the parties, performance is the discharge of those obligations, and non-performance is their failure. The three gateways measure an individual’s access to that architecture [1].

The grant lens, of record in Volumes J through O, treats the same instrument as the Emperor’s unilateral sovereign grant — a hiba — carrying a trustee structure in which the grantee holds under the grant, the qualifier appearing in the grantee’s own paper is construed against the grantee, and the instrument’s designations con-trol over later self-description [4]. Within this lens the treaty’s venue provisions — Articles 21 and 24, the consular assignment and the equal-justice guarantee, with Article 24 undivided of record [6] — do not merely describe available forums. They assign them (RECONSTRUCTION).

The reading rule of this volume is cumulative, not competitive (extension lock, §0.1.7): no proposition of this volume may be read to modify Volume I’s bilateral analysis. Where the lenses converge, the convergence is noted; where the grant lens reaches further, the surplus is labeled and argued, never asserted by stealth. Each lens keeps its own integrity, and the contrast between them is drawn on the record, under the labels.
