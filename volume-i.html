GOVERNING DOCTRINAL LOCKS

The following doctrinal constraints govern the entirety of this volume and all vol-umes within the Architecture of Peace series. No chapter, section, appendix, or analytical conclusion may be read or applied in a manner that violates these locks.

1. Treaty Text Supremacy. The text of the Treaty of Peace and Friendship (1786–87/1836) is the controlling authority. No domestic statute, judicial opin-ion, or executive action may override, reinterpret, or diminish the treaty’s pro-visions as analyzed herein.

2. No Modernization. Treaty language is reproduced and analyzed in its orig-inal form. No term has been updated, softened, or reframed to conform to contemporary usage.

3. No Editorial Commentary. The analysis presented is structural and doctrinal.

No moral, political, or policy-based editorializing has been introduced.

4. Empire of Morocco. The Moroccan signatory is referred to by its treaty-designated name: the Empire of Morocco. No alternative designation is used or implied.

5. Self-Activating Doctrine. The treaty’s protective provisions are treated as self-activating—operative upon the occurrence of the conditions they address, without requiring fresh legislative enactment or judicial adoption.

6. Sealed Content. The scholarly content of this volume is sealed upon publica-tion. No modification is permitted without an explicit version increment and re-certification.

CHAPTER 1 The Treaty Gateway Framework 1.1 Introduction

The Treaty of Peace and Friendship between the United States of America and the Empire of Morocco, signed in 1786 and ratified in 1787, established a durable ar-chitecture of mutual obligation between the two signatory powers. This architecture was not aspirational; it was operational. Its provisions created enforceable pathways through which subjects and citizens of either nation could access the protections guaranteed by the treaty instrument. These pathways are here termed gateways.

A gateway, in the doctrinal sense employed throughout this volume, is a structural precondition that must be satisfied before treaty protections can be invoked, applied, or enforced. The treaty does not operate automatically upon every person within a given territory. Rather, it operates upon persons who can demonstrate access to the treaty through one or more of the gateways described below. The treaty’s ar-chitecture is therefore conditional: protections follow from gateway access, and the denial or collapse of gateway access results in the withdrawal of treaty protections, whether by design or by external interference.

1.2 The Three Gateways

The treaty’s operational architecture can be distilled into three gateways, each of which governs a distinct dimension of treaty access.

Architecture of Peace — Volume I Gateway One: Nationality. The first and most fundamental gateway is nationality.

The treaty binds the United States of America and the Empire of Morocco as co-equal sovereign parties. Its protections extend to subjects of the Empire of Morocco and citizens of the United States, respectively. A person who can establish Moroc-can subjecthood possesses the foundational prerequisite for invoking the treaty’s protective provisions. Without nationality, the treaty instrument has no application to the individual. Nationality is therefore the gateway upon which all subsequent treaty operations depend.

Gateway Two: Public Record. The second gateway is the public record. Treaty protections do not operate in silence.
