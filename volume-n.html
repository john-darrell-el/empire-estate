Preface

This volume asks one question and answers it from the trustee’s own pages:

how has the government of the United States — the trustee of record of the 1836 treaty with the Empire of Morocco — carried that instrument, and the 1786 instrument it renewed, across its own institutional memory from 1786 to 2026?

The method is documentary evidence only.

The volume engages what the United States itself published, listed, certified, adjudicated, and commemorated:

the controlling editions (Miller, 1931 and 1934), the compilation chain (1846– 1972), the annual in-force lists (1929–2026), the diplomatic correspondence, the statutes and reports, the judicial and bibliographic registers, and the Depart-ment’s own standing characterizations. It asserts nothing doctrinal. Doctrine is reserved to Volumes L, M, and O of this series; the remedial path is reserved to Volume O.

Three conventions govern the text. First, engagement levels are stated at every locus: page image outranks direct document, which outranks the text layer, which outranks the search layer; where a search was executed and returned nothing, the volume records a certified statement of the documented search, not a claim of non-existence.

Second, flagged materials are marked at the point of citation: secondary scholarship, tertiary transcriptions, and litigation documents are engaged only as what they are. Third, quotations are rationed.

The volume quotes only where a quotation carries load, at short spans verified against page images; everything else is paraphrased with locus citations.

The volume’s own findings are stated in its chapters and nowhere resolved silently: where the transmitted record carries a citation that no primary locus supports, the volume displays the discrepancy and corrects nothing in another’s text.

Chapter 1 The Hunter Miller Note as Foundational Opening Source

§1.1.

The 1931 Hunter Miller publication in its institutional context

In 1931 the United States Government Printing Oﬀice issued the second volume of Treaties and Other International Acts of the United States of America, edited by Hunter Miller and covering Documents 1 through 40, from 1776 to 1818.1 The volume is the oﬀicial treaty publication of the United States Department of State for the founding-period corpus: the controlling federal publication of record in which the United States fixed, in permanent and citable form, the texts and the editorial history of its earliest international instruments. Within that volume, the instrument sealed at Morocco on June 23, 1786, appears as Document 14, under the heading “Morocco: June 28 and July 15, 1786,” with the running corner references “Treaty Series, No. 244” and “8 Statutes at Large, 100” — the edition’s own cross-references to the two earlier oﬀicial publication chains in which the instrument had already appeared.2 Document 14 is not a bare text.

The edition presents the instrument as a documentary complex, and the architecture of that presentation is itself evi-dence. The headnote records that the instrument was sealed with the seal of the Emperor of Morocco on June 23, 1786 (25 Shaban, A.H. 1200) and delivered to Thomas Barclay, the American agent, on June 28, 1786 (1 Ramadan, A.H.

1200); that its original is in Arabic; that an additional article was signed and sealed at Morocco on behalf of Morocco on July 15, 1786; and that a separate

§1.2. Hunter Miller as State Department treaty editor — insti-tutional capacity and authority

David Hunter Miller came to the editorship as an institutional appointment, not an avocation. The September 1929 number of the Foreign Service Journal recorded the event in terms that fix both the oﬀice and the man: a new position, “the position of Editor of the Treaties,” had just been created in the Oﬀice of the Historical Adviser, and David Hunter Miller — described in the notice as internationally known as a lawyer and student of international affairs — would assume its duties the following month.11 The same notice situated the oﬀice in its lineage: Miller succeeded to a function in the Department “made famous by J. C. Bancroft Davis,” the American agent in the Alabama Claims arbitration, whose treaty collection and whose editorial notes had been, for two generations, the standard apparatus through which the United States read its own instruments.12 The oﬀice itself was new, but its functions were, as the notice observed, among the oldest in the Department of State. First, the editorial notes in the 1931 volume are institutional acts. They were written by the Department’s Editor of the Treaties, working within the Department’s own historical oﬀice, on the Department’s own records, and published under federal authority. When the note on Document 14 speaks, the trustee speaks in its own oﬀicial record — not in a memoir, not in a private letter, not in a scholarly journal where a retrac-tion might be expected to follow, but in the controlling edition the institution itself maintains.

Second, the editor possessed institutional knowledge.

The note’s citations to the Department of State file, to the Continental Congress papers, to the Jefferson Papers, and to the consular despatches show an edi-tor working from the primary custody of the documents, not from secondary accounts.15 Third, the publication is permanent in the way that matters for evidence: printed, distributed, cited for a century, and never corrected.

A statement so made is documentary permanence of the highest institutional or-der.

This is the answer, in advance, to the objection that the note is “just an editor’s opinion.” An editor’s opinion printed by the Government Printing Oﬀice, under the direction of the Secretary of State, by authority of Congress, in the oﬀicial edition of record, is an institutional admission. The oﬀice, the authority, and the permanence are matters of public record.

Nor was Miller’s a transient incumbency of which the institution might plau-sibly wash its hands.
