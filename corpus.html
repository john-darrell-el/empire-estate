Presentation of Record

What this volume is. This is the corpus volume of the linked pair fixed and ap-proved of record: a documentary sourcebook of the instruments the United States entered or relied upon in Rights of Nationals of the United States of America in Morocco (France v. United States), International Court of Justice, judgment of 27 August 1952 — the Empire of Morocco–France treaties of 1631 and 1682; the Em-pire of Morocco–Great Britain treaties of 1721, 1750, 1751, 1760, 1801, and the Gen-eral Treaty of 1856; the Empire of Morocco–Spain treaties of 1767, 1799, and 1861; the Netherlands treaty of 1777; the United States–Empire of Morocco instruments of 1786 and 1836; and the General Act of Algeciras of 1906 — together with the correspondence spine of 1785–1836 in which the American instrument lived its working life, the 1952 judgment that construed the corpus, and the termination arc of 1914–1956 that closed the donee’s machinery. Its companion, the doctrine volume, supplies the governing-law analysis — the Mālikī hiba doctrine applied to the sealed Arabic. The linkage is explicit and of record: the corpus supplies the instruments and the commentary spine; the doctrine volume supplies the governing-law analysis; cross-references, not duplication.

Why the corpus is incorporated material, not context (the three reasons of record). First: the Court construed the United States treaty by reference to the cor-pus — its construction of the word “dispute” ran through the Empire of Morocco’s other treaties from 1631 to 1801; the corpus is the Court’s own interpretive appa-ratus. Second: the most-favoured-nation clauses of the American instrument (Ar-ticles 14 and 24) make the corpus internal to the grant’s own terms — the broader defendant-jurisdiction the United States claimed in 1952 was borrowed from the British 1856 and Spanish 1861 capitulatory treaties, never granted in the 1786/1836 text itself (the six-to-five holding of record). Third: the contrast proves the distinct structure — the capitulatory model, under which the foreign consul judges his own national as defendant in all cases, against the 1786/1836 allocation, under which the consul judges his own nationals among themselves (Article 20) and the Em-pire’s Sacred-Law tribunal takes the mixed killing/wounding matters (Article 21) — in the period law the article designates, private and public (civil and criminal)

in one. A sourcebook that printed the American instrument alone would conceal all three.

The source discipline, stated once. Pre-1836 sources of the highest credibil-ity carry the instruments and their working life; pre-1786 sources are preferred for the state of the law at the sealing. Post-1836 material enters in four warranted classes only: (a) the post-1836 event is itself the object of study — the 1856 and 1861 corpus treaties as the Court construed them, the Act of Algeciras 1906, the 1952 judgment and its pleadings, the 1956 relinquishment and Public Law 856; (b) continuity witnesses; (c) modern carriers of pre-1836 texts — Hunter Miller’s Treaty volumes (GPO 1931/1934) carrying the Arabic facsimiles, the Statutes at Large printings carrying the translations of record, the American State Papers: Foreign Relations vols. 3–6, Knox’s Naval Documents, the Founders Online tran-scriptions of the Washington correspondence; (d) finding aids and bibliography — the McLaughlin report, the archival series map — never cited for regime content. Every staged file is hash-fixed; the apparatus prints the hashes.

The boundary labels. Every claim in this volume carries one of three labels, never merged: [FACT] — document-proven, with a pin to a staged page; [AUTHOR-ITY] — the meaning a period authority carries, quoted verbatim with its pin; [THE-ORY] — the author’s analytical construction, labeled as such wherever it appears. Chapter 1 The 1786 sealed book

[FACT] The instrument as a physical object. The 1786 treaty exists as a sealed Arabic original, described in the official United States edition as “literally a book, in leather covers, with the text running from the back leaf on alternate pages” (Hunter Miller (ed.), Treaties and Other International Acts of the United States of America, vol. 2 (GPO 1931), Document 14, apparatus). It is reproduced page for page in that edition: facsimile print pages 187–211 (full-scan PDF pages 218–242; the page map of record is PDF = print + 31), preceded by the seal page at print 186 (PDF 217) — the Emperor’s round seal above the six-line Maghrebi block, the cen-

tral panel reading Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ismāʿīl … al-Ḥasanī. The Emperor

sealed alone: “the sealing on behalf of the Emperor … with the delivery thereof,

was deemed a finality on the part of Morocco. The theory seems to have been somewhat similar to our notion of a unilaterally executed grant and its delivery, for the text in the ‘book’ was not signed or sealed on behalf of the other party” (Miller apparatus, vol. 2, Document 14).
