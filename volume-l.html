Chapter 1 Why the Grantor’s Own Law Matters

The grantor declared the instrument a hiba; the grantee acknowledged it as a sovereign grant across one hundred and forty-five years of sovereign practice. Both the grantor’s own law and the grantee’s own law treat category as settled by these declarations. The remainder of this volume is the unfolding of that single doctrinal fact.

This is the doctrinal spine of Volume L. It is not a proposition the volume sets out to prove; it is the starting point from which the volume’s analysis proceeds. The category of the instrument — whether it is a unilateral sovereign grant of the kind the grantor’s tradition calls hiba, or something else — was fixed at execution by the grantor’s own declaration, and ratified across the subsequent long period by the grantee’s own acknowledgments in sovereign practice. Category-settlement occurred through acts that each of the two legal traditions in play independently recognizes as category-settling. The category question is, as between the parties whose acts deter-mine it, closed.

What the volume undertakes is not the re-opening of that closed question by a later analyst but the closing of a specific class of reopening arguments by hostile readers. A reader who accepts every proposition of Volume K and every proposition of Volume L Parts I and II may nonetheless wish to treat the category question as an open interpretive matter that later readers may re-decide on their own reasoning and their own evidence. The volume’s task is to show why that reopening is doctrinally unavailable. Each of the two traditions, operating through its own rules, makes the category question a matter the parties themselves have closed by their own acts. Any later attempt to reopen the matter is an attempt by a non-party to overrule the settled declarations of the parties whose declarations the law recognizes as controlling.

The rest of Part I develops the grantor’s own legal tradition. Part II develops the convergence of that tradition with the Western period-authority stack of Volume K. Part III applies the combined framework to the specific historical record. Every chap-ter of the volume is in service of a single purpose: showing, from two independent traditions, why no later reading can overturn the category the two sovereigns have together settled. The spine is the settled fact; the chapters are the demonstration that the settled fact stays settled.

WHY THE GRANTOR’S OWN LAW MATTERS

Every sovereign grant carries two legal traditions within it. The grantor executes the grant under the law of the grantor’s own jurisdiction. The grantee receives the grant under the law of the grantee’s own jurisdiction. Between the moment of execu-tion and the moment of reception, the grant crosses a juridical boundary. What the grant is, in its character and in its force, is determined at the moment of execution — under the grantor’s law. What the grantee may do with the grant, in its reception and its custody, is determined under the grantee’s law. The two laws are not inter-changeable, and they are not of equal authority over the instrument. The grantor’s law determines the grant’s nature; the grantee’s law determines only the grantee’s relationship to it.

Most scholarship on sovereign-to-sovereign grants proceeds asymmetrically. Where the grantor and the grantee stand in different legal traditions, commentary tends to examine the tradition of the grantee — often because the grantee’s tradition is the commentator’s own, often because the grantee’s archive is more accessible, sometimes because the grantor’s tradition is treated as an object of description rather than as a source of governing rules. The asymmetry is not innocuous. It reproduces in scholarship the precise distortion that sovereignty doctrine condemns in diplomacy: the substitution of the recipient’s law for the source’s law in the analysis of what the source conveyed.
